## Themes

Several papers focus on improving retrieval quality for RAG. LSP‑RAG introduces multi‑perspective soft‑prompt scoring for multi‑hop knowledge‑graph retrieval using lightweight models and outperforms larger‑model baselines on several KG‑QA benchmarks [P2]. TUSI presents a graph‑based retrieval baseline that lifts Recall@5 by up to 8 % compared with dense retrieval on a Persian legal citation‑grounded benchmark, highlighting the benefit of structure‑aware retrieval for domain‑specific grounding [P4]. Both works emphasize that richer relational signals (latent scoring perspectives or legal citation graphs) can compensate for smaller model sizes.

In contrast, AutoRAGBench evaluates how well LLM agents can autonomously construct and iteratively refine entire RAG pipelines. It shows that GPT‑6.1 Sol with Codex CLI reaches an average task score of 56.1, outperforming a human‑SOTA baseline, but also reveals that development‑score improvements do not always translate to higher hidden‑test accuracy, and some builds regress [P1]. This paper shifts the focus from retrieval modules alone to end‑to‑end system design and automated engineering.

A third line of work, KnowledgeClaw, moves beyond retrieval to the broader problem of omni‑modal knowledge engineering, building a typed hypergraph that stays current under edits and supports multi‑granular queries. 

Overall, the set reveals a split between (i) methods that enhance retrieval signals within fixed pipelines (LSP‑RAG, TUSI) and (ii) approaches that automate or restructure the whole RAG system (AutoRAGBench, KnowledgeClaw). The former report concrete retrieval‑metric gains, whereas the latter expose engineering trade‑offs such as cost, development time, and the disconnect between iterative scores and final answer accuracy.

## [P1] AutoRAGBench: A Benchmark for LLM Agents Automating RAG System Development

Hang Zhang, Yuzhi Pan, Ziyao Zhong, Chaokun Wang et al. · [arXiv 2611.25582v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25582v1) · relevance 0.57

**TL;DR:** AutoRAGBench shows that LLM agents can autonomously build and iteratively improve retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) systems, achieving an average score of 56.1, but gains vary and some builds regress.

**Problem**  
Developing effective RAG systems demands substantial engineering effort and iterative experimentation, and design choices differ across tasks and domains [1]. The paper asks how well LLM agents can automate this development process from scratch.  

**Method**  
The authors introduce AutoRAGBench, the first benchmark that evaluates LLM agents’ ability to create RAG pipelines in an open code space across nine distinct tasks [1][2]. Each agent receives the task corpus, training examples, a single H800 GPU, and a six‑hour time budget, and must build the system without starter code, prescribed pipelines, or persistent memory, while the development trajectory is recorded and audited for rule violations [4]. Nine agent configurations are evaluated, and both the final RAG system performance and the iterative development process are measured [2][4].  

**Key results**  
- GPT‑6.1 Sol with Codex CLI attains the highest average score of 56.1, surpassing the composite Human‑SOTA baseline average of 44.4 [1].  
- When initialized from a human‑designed system on seven tasks, the same agent improves scores by an average of 16.6 points [1].  
Across 77 builds with comparable development scores, 72 reach scores at least one point above their first version, and the median first‑to‑last gain is 5.5 points; 20 builds have last observed development scores at least one point below their best [2].  
- Larger development gains do not always translate to higher hidden‑test answer accuracy [2].  
- API cost analysis shows GPT‑5.6 Sol incurs the highest total cost (≈ $487) while GPT‑6 Sol costs ≈ $450 [5].  

**Limitations**  
- Gains in development scores are not consistently linked to improvements in hidden‑test accuracy, and some builds regress after earlier improvements [2].  
The benchmark uses a six‑hour development limit for agents, while human‑designed baselines are given a 24‑hour limit [5].  
The study evaluates nine tasks and five answering models [7].

## [P2] LSP-RAG: Latent Scoring Perspectives for Knowledge Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Haochen Liu, Guanghui Min, Song Wang, Yada Zhu et al. · [arXiv 2611.25229v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25229v1) · relevance 0.44

**TL;DR:** LSP‑RAG introduces a lightweight, multi‑perspective soft‑prompt scoring framework that enables accurate multi‑hop KG retrieval for question answering, outperforming larger‑model baselines on several KG‑QA benchmarks.

**Problem**  
Current KG‑RAG approaches either depend on costly large language models (e.g., ChatGPT) for retrieval or suffer from poor retrieval quality with lightweight models, especially in multi‑hop scenarios where heterogeneous relevance signals and contextual coherence are needed [1][2].  

**Method**  
LSP‑RAG augments lightweight language models with a multi‑perspective prompted scoring module that uses (1) perspective‑specific soft prompts to create distinct scoring functions for candidate triplets and (2) a contextual soft prompt that evolves with each reasoning hop to incorporate previously explored evidence [2][3][4]. Retrieval uses K = 3 latent scoring perspectives, a hop budget of H = 1 for MetaQA‑1hop and H = 2 for other datasets, and caps the number of retrieved triplets at 20 per query [6]. The retrieval stage uses lightweight models (LLaMA2‑7B, LLaMA3‑8B, Flan‑T5‑3B), while ChatGPT‑3.5 is used only during the final answer generation stage for some baselines [5][6].  

**Key results**  
LSP‑RAG was evaluated on four KG‑QA benchmarks—WebQuestionsSP, WikiMovies, and MetaQA (1‑hop and 2‑hop) [5][6]. The paper reports that LSP‑RAG even surpasses ChatGPT‑based retrieval despite using only lightweight models, achieving higher accuracy and lower inference cost [1][6]. No specific numerical scores or percentage gains are provided in the excerpts.  

**Limitations**  
The provided excerpts do not detail any explicit limitations of LSP‑RAG; such information is absent from the available text.

## [P3] KnowledgeClaw: Towards Agentic Knowledge Engineering via Omnimodal Hypergraphs

Haoran Luo, Jiapu Wang, Linhao Luo, Luu Anh Tuan · [arXiv 2611.24294v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24294v1) · relevance 0.36

**TL;DR:** KnowledgeClaw is an agentic system that builds and maintains an omnimodal typed hypergraph for live knowledge bases, outperforming 30 baselines on the OmniKE‑Bench suite.

**Problem** – Existing knowledge‑engineering systems handle at most two of the three requirements for a live knowledge base: omnimodality, a closed‑loop lifecycle, and multi‑granular application, and no benchmark evaluates all three together [1].  

**Method** – The authors formalize Omnimodal Knowledge Engineering (OmniKE) and introduce OmniKE‑Bench, a benchmark with six model‑free tasks covering 1,829 items from 3,960 real files in five modalities and ten personas [2][3]. KnowledgeClaw reads every file natively into an omnimodal typed hypergraph (C1), keeps the graph current under edits via a closed‑loop lifecycle agent (C2), and routes each query to the appropriate granularity (C3) [1][3].  

**Key results** – Against 30 baselines, KnowledgeClaw ranks first on all six OmniKE‑Bench tasks and on all ten personas, achieving an overall OKE score of 55.9, which is +14.6 points higher than the strongest baseline [1][3]. The strongest baseline still trails by 5.2 OKE points [3]. The system remains current after every edit, demonstrating successful lifecycle maintenance [3].  

The benchmark provides limited evaluation for audio and video modalities, with only three items involving these media, and the paper poses research questions about efficiency and robustness [8][2].

## [P4] TUSI: A Citation-Grounded Graph-Based Benchmark

Marzieh Karimi, Mehran Safayani, Alireza Basiri · [arXiv 2611.21823v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21823v1) · relevance 0.34

**TL;DR:** TUSI introduces a Persian legal benchmark with citation‑grounded QA and multiple‑choice tasks, and shows that a graph‑based retrieval model boosts recall by up to 8 % over dense retrieval.

**Problem**: Evaluating retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) systems for Persian law is difficult because existing benchmarks are either synthetically generated from statutes or derived from informal Q&A sources without explicit links to authoritative legal documents, limiting realistic assessment of grounded legal reasoning [1][2].

**Method**: The authors construct TUSI‑Bench on a multi‑layered legal knowledge framework that links 7,261 statutory articles, expert interpretations, doctrinal writings, and Supreme Court Unification Rulings into a legal knowledge graph; they create two complementary tasks—TUSI‑QA with 1,999 real lawyer‑client questions requiring explicit citations, and TUSI‑Bar with approximately 500 four‑option Bar‑exam questions—and propose a structure‑aware retrieval baseline that leverages the graph’s relational structure [1][2][4].

**Key results**:  
- The graph‑based retrieval baseline improves Recall@5 by up to 8 percentage points over its dense‑retrieval counterpart [2].  
- After iterative filtering, the mean answer‑to‑legal‑provision similarity increases from 0.526 to 0.556 (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.36), with a comparable improvement for question‑to‑provision similarity [4].  
- Coverage analysis shows 27.7 % of articles have explanation annotations and 33.6 % have reference links, while the out‑degree distribution follows a power‑law (α ≈ 2.50, R² = 0.961) [3].  
- The benchmark comprises 7,261 legal articles, 1,999 open‑ended QA items, and roughly 500 multiple‑choice items [1][2].

**Limitations**: The provided excerpts do not state any explicit limitations of the benchmark or the experiments [7].

## [P5] StringQFT: A Multiuse Textbook Resource for AI-Assisted Theoretical Physics

Xingyang Yu · [arXiv 2611.23225v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23225v1) · relevance 0.32

**TL;DR:** StringQFT is a comprehensive, AI‑compatible textbook collection for string‑theoretic constructions of quantum field theory, offering extensive retrieval and tool‑use infrastructure but providing no reported performance metrics.

**Problem** – Learning and connecting the diverse string‑theory constructions that underlie quantum field theory is challenging because it requires familiarity with many degrees of freedom, conventions, and calculational tools, and a single physical question can appear in multiple string‑theoretic languages [2]. The authors aim to integrate these routes into a unified textbook resource and to enable AI‑assisted use of such material [1][2].  

**Method** – The authors assembled a textbook collection comprising 18 volumes, 190 chapters, and 3,795 pages that cover topics such as brane probes, geometric engineering, and F‑theory [1][3]. The collection includes an “open‑book benchmark inventory” of 1,073 items (499 with automatic finite‑answer checking) and 1,371 section records linked to PDF locations for retrieval‑augmented generation [1][6]. Training‑data preparation is illustrated with 21 private, source‑linked examples, and 19 calculation tools with specified inputs and finite domains are exposed for agent use [1][6]. The resource is organized into foundational volumes (I–V) and subject‑specific blocks (e.g., brane probes, F‑theory) to guide graduate‑level readers [3][4].  

**Key results** – The resource contains:  
- 18 volumes, 190 chapters, 3,795 pages [1];  
- 1,073 benchmark items, of which 499 support automatic finite‑answer checking [1];  
- 1,371 section records for retrieval‑augmented generation [1];  
- 21 private, source‑linked examples for training‑data preparation [1];  
- 19 calculation tools with defined input domains [1];  
- 499 public questions evaluated automatically and 573 manual questions assessed by humans [5];  
- Demonstrated retrieval of a specific passage (record V.8.3) with correct PDF anchoring [6];  
- Successful execution of a bounded calculation workflow on a fresh Python environment [7].  
No quantitative scores (e.g., accuracy, BLEU, retrieval recall) or comparative baselines are reported in the excerpts.  

**Limitations** – The authors note that the retriever returns passages but does not measure retrieval quality over a representative query set, nor does it assess answer faithfulness or the performance of downstream generated‑answer systems [6]. No hosted question‑answering service is provided, and the tool interfaces do not supply answers for problems outside their declared domains, leaving model planning, learning, and research outcomes unmeasured [7].
