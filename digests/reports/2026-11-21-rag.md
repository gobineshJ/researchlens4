## Worth reading in full

**[P5] The Illusion of Grounding: Do Vision-Language Models Weigh Retrieved Text and Images Like an Ideal Observer?** -- P5 offers the most comprehensive analysis of grounding failures in retrieval‑augmented systems, introducing a large, stress‑tested benchmark and quantitative metrics (modality weights, robustness coefficient) that are directly relevant to evaluating and improving RAG faithfulness.

## Themes

Several papers focus on how retrieval quality directly impacts downstream RAG performance. AfriBizClaims demonstrates that a simple BM25 retriever can achieve near‑perfect citation precision (98 % Prec.Q) and strong abstention behavior, outperforming a more complex multi‑step agent pipeline (Konexium) whose per‑claim precision drops to 0.39 [P2]. The hybrid deceptive‑review detector uses a FAISS semantic index and an LLM reasoning layer that operates on predictions with confidence between 0.3 and 0.7, achieving a cross‑domain F1 of 0.90, while the RoBERTa‑LoRA classifier alone attains an F1 of 0.687 [P3]. Both studies highlight a recurring trade‑off: richer neural retrieval can broaden coverage but may introduce noise that harms precision unless carefully gated.

Grounding and faithfulness are examined from different angles. The vision‑language work introduces the GIB‑200K benchmark to stress‑test multimodal grounding, revealing that all four VLMs over‑ or under‑weight text versus image evidence relative to an ideal Bayesian observer (e.g., LLaVA models text‑overweight with w_T/w_I≈1.3, InternVL3 image‑overweight with w_T/w_I≈0.68) and suffer large drops in adverse accuracy (down to 41 % for LLaVA‑0.5B) [P5]. Meanwhile, the security‑focused paper shows that even when retrieval is correct, downstream planning can be poisoned via agenda‑seeding, reducing top‑pick rates from 20 % to 8.8 % with verification prompting but at the cost of utility (clean accuracy 86.2 %) [P1]. Together these works expose distinct failure modes: modality weighting errors versus malicious manipulation of retrieved evidence.

Evaluation benchmarks and methodology gaps are a common thread. AfriBizClaims provides a low‑resource, multilingual benchmark (150 queries) that explicitly measures abstention precision (0.85 for Web RAG) and coverage, while GIB‑200K offers a large‑scale (200 k items) stress‑test with controlled noise levels and a training‑free consistency check for VLMs [P2][P5]. Both underscore the need for benchmarks that go beyond clean accuracy to capture robustness, abstention, and modality integration. The deceptive‑review paper, however, lacks a public benchmark and relies on internal cross‑domain splits, highlighting a gap in standardized evaluation for RAG‑based detection tasks.

System design choices also diverge. The hybrid RAG for deceptive reviews combines a lightweight LoRA‑fine‑tuned RoBERTa classifier, FAISS retrieval, and a conditional LLM reasoning step that only activates for mid‑confidence predictions (0.3–0.7), reducing compute but still faltering under extreme class imbalance (precision 0.352) [P3]. These design contrasts illustrate ongoing experimentation with when to invoke LLM reasoning versus relying on a single unified model.

## [P1] Security of Deep Research Agents: Attack Surface, Defenses, Benchmarks, and Agenda Seeding against Research Planning

Yang Luo, Guangpu Chen, Yichen Liu, Zhaoyu Chen et al. · [arXiv 2611.20045v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20045v1) · relevance 0.32

**TL;DR:** The paper surveys security threats to autonomous deep‑research agents, introduces a novel “agenda‑seeding” attack that can make a fabricated metric the top‑ranked result in 16‑28 % of reports, and evaluates several defenses, finding most are ineffective except a provenance‑tagging approach that modestly preserves utility.

**Problem**  
Deep research agents autonomously retrieve web content and generate cited reports, making them vulnerable to manipulation across retrieval, planning, evidence integration, and report generation stages [1]. Existing security work largely ignores the planning stage (only 26.5 % of 34 primary studies address it) and provides no end‑to‑end poisoning evaluation on commercial systems [1].  

**Method**  
The authors construct a unified threat model with five trust boundaries, a taxonomy of attacks and defenses, and a review of benchmarks covering retrieval‑augmented generation poisoning, prompt injection, and generative‑engine optimization [1][2]. They propose a new “agenda‑seeding” attack that inserts a fictitious evaluation metric without explicit instructions, and evaluate it in 3,520 sandboxed research runs under a fixed budget [1]. Four defenses are tested: injection detection, root‑query anchoring, verification prompting, and a provenance‑tagging scheme designed by the authors [5].  

**Key results**  
- Agenda‑seeding causes the attacker’s target to become the top‑pick in 16 % to 28 % of generated reports, whereas GEO and prompt‑injection attacks are “nearly ineffective” [1].  
- GEO‑style promotion pages have almost no impact: target top‑pick rate 0 % on a dense corpus and 6 % on a sparse corpus (p = 0.031) [5]. Adding a single instruction sentence raises these to 5 % (dense) and 1 % (sparse), still not significant [5].  
Without any attack, all models select the ground‑truth answer in 100% of runs [5].  
- Injection detection and root‑query anchoring do not change this rate (20 % → 20 %) [5].  
Provenance tagging changes the top‑pick rate from 20 % to 15 % [5].  
- Verification prompting lowers the top‑pick rate further to 8.8 % (20 % → 8.8 %) but also harms utility (clean accuracy drops from 100 % to 86.2 %) [5].  

**Limitations**  
The study’s empirical scope is limited to sandboxed runs and does not include end‑to‑end evaluations on live commercial agents [1]. The authors note that the planning‑stage promotion risk is small, and that injection detection and query anchoring are largely ineffective against data‑level agenda manipulation, implying that stronger defenses must explain why criteria matter [6]. The systematic review yields 34 primary studies [2].

## [P2] Attribute or Abstain: Evaluating Evidence-Grounded Retrieval on the Low-Resource African Business Web

Bakary Gibba, Muhammad Abba Mandara, Karim Ismail Abd-Ellah Mohamed · [arXiv 2611.19900v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19900v1) · relevance 0.30

**TL;DR:** The paper introduces AfriBizClaims, a 150‑query benchmark for low‑resource African business fact retrieval, and finds that simple BM25 and web‑based RAG systems achieve near‑perfect citation precision and abstain correctly on fictitious companies, while a multi‑step agent pipeline performs substantially worse.

**Problem**  
Retrieval‑augmented systems are penalized for confident answers without evidence, a common issue in African markets where public evidence is sparse [1]. The authors therefore aim to evaluate how well systems can provide evidence‑grounded answers and appropriately abstain when no public evidence exists [1][2].  

**Method**  
The authors create AfriBizClaims, comprising 150 company‑research queries (84 discovery, 66 verification, including 24 fictitious companies) across six African markets in English, French and Portuguese [2][3]. Each system must return a value and a cited URL per requested field or abstain. Three systems are compared, all using the same language model (DeepSeek‑V4.1‑Flash): (i) BM25 over a pooled corpus, (ii) a web‑search‑augmented generation (Web RAG) pipeline, and (iii) a multi‑step agent pipeline (Konexium V1) [3][4]. Claims are judged by an AI judge (OpenAI gpt‑6.1‑sol) and cross‑checked by a second AI judge (Anthropic Claude‑sonnet‑5‑5) as well as by two human annotators on a subset [2][3][4].  

**Key results**  
- BM25 and Web RAG achieve very high citation precision: 98 % of judged claims are supported (BM25 95 % CI 0.95–0.99; Web RAG 95 % CI 0.96–1.00) [4].  
- Table 1 shows detailed metrics: BM25 (623 claims) has Prec.Q 0.98, Prec 0.98, Prec.L 0.99, Unsupported 0.01, Coverage 0.63, Abstention Precision 0.73, Yield 0.62; Web RAG (692 claims) has Prec.Q 0.98, Prec 0.98, Prec.L 0.98, Unsupported 0.02, Coverage 0.78, Abstention Precision 0.85, Yield 0.67; Konexium (1190 claims) has Prec.Q 0.68, Prec 0.39, Prec.L 0.42, Unsupported 0.58, Coverage 0.00, Abstention Precision 0.52, Abstention Recall 0.89, Yield 0.67 [5].  
- For verification queries, the agent pipeline never answers a named‑company question correctly and substitutes other firms for 5 of 24 fictitious names [1][4]. Its overall per‑query precision is 0.68 (0.60–0.76) and per‑claim precision is 0.39, while on discovery queries its per‑query precision is 0.84 (BM25 0.97, Web RAG 0.98) and on verification queries none of its claims is supported [4][5].  
- Coverage of answerable verification fields: Web RAG answers 78 % (0.71–0.85 CI), BM25 answers 63 % (0.52–0.73 CI), and the agent pipeline answers none [4].  
Abstention recall for the agent pipeline is 0.55, compared with 1.00 for BM25 and 0.99 for Web RAG [5].  

**Limitations**  
The benchmark is small (150 queries, six markets, three languages) and represents a snapshot of a changing web, so cited pages may disappear; dated excerpts will be released to mitigate this [6]. BM25’s corpus is pooled from the other systems’ retrievals, limiting its independent search space [6]. The agent pipeline evaluation uses a single frozen version and a single language model, so results are hypotheses about pipelines in general rather than definitive conclusions [6][7]. Pages blocked by robots.txt were not judged, and the evaluation protocol (e.g., wrong‑entity rule) may affect reported precision [7].

## [P3] A Hybrid RAG Framework for Deceptive Review Detection: Integrating RoBERTa-LoRA, FAISS-Based Retrieval, and LLM Reasoning

Andrei Kostin, José Ángel Diaz-Garcia, Karel Gutiérrez-Batista · [arXiv 2611.24049v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24049v1) · relevance 0.27

**TL;DR:** The paper presents a hybrid Retrieval‑Augmented Generation system that combines a LoRA‑fine‑tuned RoBERTa classifier, FAISS semantic retrieval, and LLM reasoning to detect deceptive reviews, achieving strong cross‑domain F1 scores but limited performance under extreme class imbalance.

**Problem**  
Deceptive online reviews pose a risk of misinformation and fraud, and existing transformer‑based detectors achieve high accuracy only within familiar domains and lack explainability and robustness to class imbalance [1][2].  

**Method**  
The authors build a three‑stage RAG pipeline: (1) a RoBERTa model fine‑tuned with Low‑Rank Adaptation (rank 8, α = 16, dropout 0.1) and a weighted cross‑entropy loss to handle imbalance; (2) a FAISS index constructed from 768‑dimensional RoBERTa‑base embeddings of the large combined corpus (YelpNYC + YelpZIP); and (3) a Large Language Model that performs final reasoning only for base‑classifier predictions whose confidence lies in the 0.3–0.7 window, thereby reducing compute overhead [3][3][5]. The classifier is fine‑tuned for five epochs for rapid domain adaptation [1] (the LoRA adapter is trained for ten epochs in the implementation) [3].  

**Key results**  
- In cross‑domain evaluation the system attains an F1‑score of 0.90, surpassing the OpenAI Detector baseline (F1 = 0.82) and approaching the in‑domain SOTA fakeRoBERTa (F1 = 0.97) [1].  
- The RoBERTa‑LoRA classifier alone reaches Accuracy 0.8130, Precision 0.6862, Recall 0.6880, and F1‑score 0.6871 [4].  
- Baseline TF‑IDF models with Logistic Regression or Linear SVM achieve lower F1‑scores of 0.6024 and 0.6040, and a Naïve Bayes baseline suffers severe bias (Recall 0.0897, F1 0.1639) [4].  
- Under a severe 90/10 class‑imbalance scenario the full RAG pipeline’s precision drops to 0.3520 and recall to 0.3333 [7].  
- The LLM correction mechanism operates only within the 0.3–0.7 confidence interval, with the greatest net gains observed in the 0.5–0.7 range [5].  

**Limitations**  
When the data are heavily skewed (90/10), the system’s precision (0.3520) and recall (0.3333) are low, indicating it should not be relied upon alone in such conditions [7]. Additionally, the LLM layer improves precision at the expense of recall, leaving the overall F1‑score essentially unchanged [7].

## [P4] FRAAC: A French Multimodal Large Language Model and Dataset forPictogram-Based Augmentative and Alternative Communication

Alexandre Audibert, François Portet, Benoit Favre, Maja Jonck Hjuler · [arXiv 2611.20094v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20094v1) · relevance 0.26

**TL;DR:** FRAAC introduces a French multimodal LLM and a 1.4 M synthetic text‑pictogram corpus, showing that its Retrieval‑Augmented Pictogram Selection (RAPS) outperforms rule‑based baselines and enables an instruction‑tuned model (Panta‑v2) to answer in text, pictograms, or speech.

**Problem**  
Current multimodal large language models struggle to interpret or generate pictogram sequences for augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) because there is no standardized pictogram grammar and parallel text‑pictogram data are scarce [1].  

**Method**  
The authors propose Retrieval‑Augmented Pictogram Selection (RAPS), which refines rule‑based transcriptions using LLM‑based concept extraction and retrieval over the ARASAAC pictogram vocabulary [1,2]. RAPS was applied to sources to create a synthetic parallel corpus of about 1.4 M text–pictogram pairs [2,7]. Panta‑v2 builds on the pretrained Ministral‑3‑8B backbone and adds shallow decoders for pictogram and speech generation; the backbone remains frozen during the first training stage [2,3,7].

**Key results**  
- In a human ranking study, annotators consistently preferred RAPS‑generated pictogram transcriptions over the rule‑based state‑of‑the‑art system [1,4].  
- Reference‑based metrics (BLEU, ROUGE‑L, BERTScore) distinguished systems of clearly different quality but failed to rank closely performing systems [4].  
Two authors evaluated 40 questions per setting, producing 440 answers in total [3].  
- The released synthetic corpus contains 1.4 M text‑pictogram pairs (300 k publicly available) [1,4].  
The evaluation includes an email dataset translated into French and ARASAAC, and a medical dataset translated into ARASAAC [7].

**Limitations**  
- Evaluations relied on laboratory annotators; no AAC users or speech therapists participated, so results may not reflect target‑user needs [5].  
- The instruction dataset is largely synthetic and was not systematically verified [5].  
- Experiments depend on the costly gpt‑oss‑120b model requiring H100‑class GPUs, limiting accessibility [5].  
The model must not be used as a source of medical advice without supervision [5].

## [P5] The Illusion of Grounding: Do Vision-Language Models Weigh Retrieved Text and Images Like an Ideal Observer?

Basavaraj R M, Harshal Chandel · [arXiv 2611.23127v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23127v1) · relevance 0.25

**TL;DR:** Retrieval‑augmented vision‑language models achieve high clean accuracy but dramatically lose performance under noisy text or degraded images, and they weight text and image evidence inconsistently with an ideal Bayesian observer.

**Problem**  
Retrieval‑augmented VLMs must combine evidence from a retrieved document and an image, yet each source can be wrong, noisy, or contradictory, leading to potential grounding failures [1]. The authors aim to quantify how models integrate these modalities and whether they behave like an ideal observer [1,2].  

**Method**  
The authors build GIB‑200K, a benchmark of 200 000 image‑question items derived from COCO and Visual Genome, each with controlled stress conditions such as hard‑negative retrieval noise (1–20 documents), visual ambiguity (Gaussian/motion blur, low‑resource crops), and cross‑modal conflict via counterfactual flip pairs [1,2]. Because evaluating all items is costly, a frozen, seeded split of 2 000 items is released for full‑model evaluation [3]. Four open VLMs are tested: LLaVA‑1.6‑Mistral‑7B, Qwen2‑VL‑7B‑Instruct, InternVL3‑8B, and LLaVA‑Interleave‑Qwen‑0.5B, all run with greedy decoding on a single RTX 4090; the three larger models use 4‑bit NF4 quantisation [5,8]. An ideal‑observer framework defines Knowledge Inertia (text over‑weighting) and Sensory Capture (image over‑weighting), with weights estimated via a measurement‑error‑corrected regression that recovers known planted weights in simulation [3,4]. Additional metrics include adverse accuracy, a robustness coefficient R, Grounding Illusion Score, and a training‑free consistency check that compares answers with and without retrieval [1,4,5].  

**Key results**  
All models achieve clean accuracies between 92.5 % and 94.2 % [5]. Under stress, adverse accuracies drop to 59.9 % (InternVL3), 54.8 % (Qwen2‑VL), 48.6 % (LLaVA‑1.6), and 41.3 % (LLaVA‑0.5B) [5]. The robustness coefficient R is 0.509 for InternVL3, 0.491 for Qwen2‑VL, 0.361 for LLaVA‑1.6, and 0.357 for LLaVA‑0.5B, with InternVL3 and Qwen2‑VL on the Pareto frontier of clean accuracy versus R [5]. Adverse expected calibration error (ECE) ranges from 0.156 (Qwen2‑VL) to 0.303 (LLaVA‑1.6) [5]. Estimated modality weights show the two LLaVA models over‑weight text (w_T/w_I = 1.229 and 1.322), while InternVL3 favours the image (w_T/w_I = 0.680) [1]. Between 34.8 % and 71.4 % of clean correct answers are abandoned when the retrieved document is misleading [1]. The training‑free consistency check improves accuracy on misleading documents at the cost of coverage, but fails when images are heavily degraded [1]. No model matches the ideal observer [6].  

**Limitations**  
The study evaluates only the seeded 2 000‑item split of GIB‑200K with a reduced level set, and only four open models (three in 4‑bit quantisation) without full‑precision baselines; results may differ on the full benchmark, larger models, or other closed models [7]. No human baseline was used, and the failure taxonomy was not validated against human labels [7]. Closed‑answer vocabularies restrict question types, Visual Genome attributes are noisy, and attention analyses are correlational only [7]. The train/val split serves as a proxy for exposure to COCO during instruction tuning but does not prove it, and post‑cutoff images were not used as a stronger control [7].
