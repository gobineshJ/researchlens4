## Report

**Paper 1 – RAG‑NIMBF**  
*Problem*: The authors note the need for a systematic way to analyze retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) configurations, which are currently evaluated with limited metrics and on narrow datasets [1].  
*Method*: They introduce RAG‑NIMBF, a non‑invasive, modular benchmarking framework that sweeps over SQuAD configurations while keeping top‑k and the MMR λ fixed, and evaluates multiple metrics such as lexical scores, semantic similarity, retrieval quality, and runtime [1].  
*Key results*: Across the swept configurations, prompt specificity had the strongest positive effect on faithfulness, MMR improved retrieval coverage, and chunk‑size effects were secondary; runtime measurements show that Qwen3.6‑27B‑NVFP4 can match GPT‑4o‑mini on mean faithfulness at lower latency, while the frontier reference model does not dominate on RAG‑relevant metrics [1].  
*Limitations*: The sweep excludes reranking, HyDE, and embedding‑model comparisons; it holds top‑k and MMR λ fixed; evaluation is limited to the short, extractive, single‑pass SQuAD dataset, so results may not transfer to long‑document, multi‑hop, noisy, or enterprise corpora; quality assessments are automated and relative to the RAGAS critic; prompt templates were authored once and applied uniformly, leaving open whether different model families benefit from structurally different prompts [1].

**Paper 2 – GARRET (ViDoRe‑V3‑Region)**  
*Problem*: The work addresses the challenge of retrieving layout‑aware regions from document pages for question answering without any query‑to‑region relevance labels [2].  
*Method*: GARRET is trained on 118 k page‑question‑answer triples using a persistent layout candidate set; an auxiliary vision‑language model (VLM) applies retriever‑weighted cross‑attention during training, then is removed for deployment, leaving a context‑preserving retriever that processes a single rendered page and crops region features based on canonicalized layout boxes [2].  
*Key results*: The excerpt describes the data preprocessing, candidate canonicalization, and the architecture of the context‑preserving retriever, but does not provide quantitative performance results [2].  
*Limitations*: The provided excerpt does not state explicit limitations for this paper [2].

**Paper 3 – Plant‑aware PLC Programming Copilot**  
*Problem*: Existing retrieval‑augmented code generators treat PLC coding as a one‑shot pipeline and lack persistent plant‑specific knowledge, forcing engineers to switch contexts between documentation, project files, and code editors [4].  
*Method*: The authors present a plant‑aware conversational copilot that (1) ingests plant‑specific context (I/O lists, device labels, module configuration, existing program fragments), (2) uses a chat‑first interaction model for explanation, debugging, and design discussion, and (3) offers a graduated tool‑access ladder (Chat → Code → Compile → Deploy) to keep engineers in control of real‑world effects [4].  
*Key results*: The contribution list highlights the three novel components (plant‑aware context ingestion, conversational interaction, graduated tool access), but the excerpt does not include empirical evaluation results [4].  
*Limitations*: No explicit limitations are mentioned in the excerpt [4].

**Paper 4 – DECLINECRAFT Framework**  
*Problem*: The study investigates how retrieval‑augmented LLMs handle temporal knowledge gaps and abstention, focusing on the ability to recognize missing or outdated information [5].  
*Method*: Using a controlled environment built on the Minecraft Wiki, the framework creates questions (via Qwen3.5 122B‑A10B), verifies them (Gemma 4 31B), labels abstentions and premise denials (Gemma 4 26B‑A4B), and evaluates answers with multiple LLM judges; six retrieval pipelines are compared, all using fixed‑size 1,500‑character chunks embedded with Qwen3‑Embedding‑8B [5].  
*Key results*: The authors observe that single‑shot RAG performance varies across models and that premise denial does not guarantee recognition of missing evidence; they call for future work on evidence‑sufficiency judgments and cross‑domain testing [5].  
*Limitations*: (1) Corpus limited to the Minecraft Wiki, which may not reflect other domains; (2) reliance on LLM judges that may introduce biases; (3) only one chunking scheme and a single embedding model are used; (4) premise‑denial scoring excludes certain responses; (5) evaluation covers a limited set of models due to hardware constraints, focusing on the Qwen3 family [5].

**Paper 5 – nokast‑secureRAG**  
*Problem*: The paper tackles the need for a lightweight, local defense against context‑dependent “judgment‑flip” prompt‑injection attacks in retrieval‑augmented generation systems [6].  
*Method*: A teacher→judge→student distillation pipeline trains a ∼0.5 B semantic firewall that operates as a detection‑side proxy (ASR); the student inherits judgment ability from the teacher and is evaluated on synthetic corpora with query‑intent signals [6].  
*Key results*: The distilled student achieves 0.994 recall at 0.026 FPR, outperforms a strong public prompt‑injection classifier and a keyword filter, and successfully resolves judgment‑flip cases that context‑blind baselines cannot; a negative out‑of‑distribution result on PoisonedRAG is also reported [6].  
*Limitations*: Validation is in‑distribution with a single external check; the guard is a detection‑side proxy rather than measuring downstream generation; adaptive adversaries, gradient‑based or multi‑chunk attacks, and human red‑team testing are unexamined; latency remains above the interactive target; the work is English‑only and validated on a single synthetic domain; the “suspicious” class has very few examples, making its recall noisy [6].

**Shared Themes**  
All five papers focus on retrieval‑augmented generation (RAG) systems and seek to improve or evaluate them beyond naïve pipelines. Each work highlights the importance of **contextual information**—whether prompt specificity, layout‑aware regions, plant‑specific configurations, temporal knowledge, or query intent—to achieve higher quality or safer outputs. All papers acknowledge **evaluation limitations**, often citing narrow datasets (e.g., SQuAD, Minecraft Wiki), reliance on automated or LLM‑based judges, and the need for broader domain validation. Finally, several studies propose **modular or extensible frameworks** (RAG‑NIMBF, GARRET, the plant‑aware copilot, DECLINECRAFT, nokast‑secureRAG) that can be expanded in future work to address the identified constraints.
