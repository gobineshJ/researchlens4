## [P1] RAG-FGO: Dynamic Optimization of Query-Rewriting Agents for Generative RAG

Qigan Sun, Xudong Wang, Chaoning Zhang, Chenghao Li et al. · [arXiv 2611.17788v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17788v1) · relevance 0.50

**TL;DR:** RAG-FGO is a dynamic, black‑box optimization framework that jointly tunes discrete prompt templates and continuous decoding parameters for query rewriting, yielding modest but consistent gains on diverse retrieval‑augmented generation benchmarks, though it requires offline training and lacks online adaptation.

**Problem**  
Retrieval‑augmented generation suffers from noisy retrieval feedback, difficulty jointly optimizing discrete prompts and continuous decoding controls, and poor generalization of static rewriting strategies in semantically complex scenarios [1][2]. Consequently, static or locally adjusted strategies often fail to explore the strategy space effectively and struggle with stability under noisy, non‑stationary feedback [2].

**Method**  
RAG‑FGO reformulates query rewriting as a mixed discrete‑continuous, noisy black‑box optimization problem and represents each rewriting agent by a prompt template plus decoding parameters (e.g., temperature, top‑p, repetition penalty) [2][3]. The framework conducts parallel global search (LLM‑driven prompt restructuring for large semantic jumps) and local search (fine‑grained synonym or phrasing edits) to explore and exploit the strategy space [3][4]. An elite‑preserving memory pool retains the top‑M agents across iterations, re‑evaluating them against newly generated agents to mitigate stochastic degradation and guide convergence [4]. The process iterates until a stability criterion is met or the computational budget is exhausted [4].

**Key results**  
On seven reasoning benchmarks using GPT‑4o‑mini as the generator, RAG‑FGO attains an average accuracy of 63.7 %, narrowly surpassing the next best baseline (ReFeri, 63.6 %) [6]. Individual scores include 78.0 % on MATH500, 64.5 % on MMLU‑pro, 44.1 % on GPQA, 79.2/85.0 (EM/F1) on DROP, 35.9 % on HotpotQA, 61.7 % on MuSR‑op, and 83.1 % on MuSR‑ta [6]. Compared with gradient‑free prompt‑optimization baselines under identical RAG settings, RAG‑FGO achieves higher F1 scores: 74.5 % on HotpotQA, 66.1 % on MuSiQue, 73.1 % on Natural Questions, yielding an average of 71.2 % versus 58.6–63.5 % for AutoPrompt, Genetic Prompt Search, and RLPrompt [6]. In heuristic‑optimizer comparisons (same generator LLM), RAG‑FGO records 71.5 % on LongBench, 81.5 % on HotpotQA, and 77.3 % on 2WikiMultihopQA, outperforming PSO, GA, SA, FOA, TLBO, and HS baselines [6]. The paper does not report training time, model size, or inference latency.

**Limitations**  
RAG‑FGO’s optimization is performed offline and requires an additional training budget before deployment, limiting flexibility for rapid iteration or frequent updates [7]. The current system does not support online or continual adaptation to evolving queries or changing knowledge bases, and the authors note the need for more efficient optimization schemes for such scenarios [7].

## [P2] Logically Consistent Accurate Forecasts

Kate Yuan, Rohan Alur, Manish Raghavan, Devavrat Shah · [arXiv 2611.17735v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17735v1) · relevance 0.20

**TL;DR:** Fine‑tuning a supervisory LLM with a joint loss for accuracy and logical consistency improves ForecastBench Brier scores by about 9 % and cuts structural inconsistency errors by 80‑90 % compared with a raw GPT‑5.1 forecaster.

**Problem**  
Agentic forecasting systems built on large language models often produce forecasts that violate probability axioms, making related predictions mutually inconsistent and limiting their decision‑making utility [1].

**Method**  
The authors fine‑tune a supervisory model (Qwen3.6‑35B‑A3B) on clusters of related questions, prompting the model with the text, reasoning, and upstream estimates for every question in a cluster and extracting a refined probability from the normalized “yes”/“no” token distribution [2][4].  
Training minimizes a weighted sum of three terms: (i) Brier score on observed outcomes, (ii) logical‑consistency penalties for complement, partition, and monotonicity constraints, and (iii) a proximity term penalizing deviation from the upstream forecasts [2][4].

**Key results**  
Across three random seeds, the fine‑tuned model achieves Brier scores of 0.1385, 0.1372 and 0.1369, representing relative reductions of about 8.6% to 9.8% compared with the raw forecaster [5].  
Structural inconsistency metrics improve dramatically: negation squared error drops from 0.0468 to 0.0056 (‑88.09%); partition squared error falls from 0.2237 to about 0.0218 (‑90.27%); monotonicity L1 violation declines from 0.0186 to about 0.0032 (‑82.59%) [5].  
Ablation removing the consistency loss yields a 51.68% reduction in pooled partition violation, while removing reasoning traces or cross‑question context leads to smaller Brier‑score improvements (‑2.67% and ‑8.88%, respectively) compared with the full method [6].  
The heteroskedastic empirical Bayes, log‑odds James‑Stein, and zero‑target shrinkage baselines do not meaningfully improve performance relative to the raw forecaster [7].

**Limitations**  
The approach relies on synthetic related questions whose true outcomes are unobserved, so consistency penalties are applied without direct outcome feedback [2][4].  
  
Ablations show that removing reasoning traces or cross‑question context reduces accuracy, whereas removing the consistency term slightly improves the Brier score (‑9.69% versus ‑9.32% for the full method) [6][8].
